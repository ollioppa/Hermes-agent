# Rent, fragmentation, and transmission matrices

## Claim-level ladder

1. **Existence:** Does the mechanism occur?
2. **Prevalence:** How common is it in the relevant market?
3. **Magnitude:** How much cashflow, capital, employment, or collateral is exposed?
4. **Transmission:** Does one party's loss change another party's financing or behavior?
5. **Timing:** When can those flows reach earnings, credit, employment, or prices?

Keep each conclusion at the highest evidenced rung. Do not reject a lower rung because a higher rung remains open.

## Company and ecosystem matrix

Fill these fields in order:

- **Customer job:** practical benefit bought.
- **Rent source:** product superiority, scarcity, brand, default status, network access, financing, regulation, switching cost, or externalization.
- **Phase:** build/subsidize → scale → harvest → defend → compress → restructure.
- **Support intensity:** discounts, promotions, ads, incentives, financing, longer terms, PIK, residual guarantees, or moral pressure.
- **Lifetime pool:** initial sale → finance/lease → service/parts → software/subscription/ads → secondary market/replacement.
- **External ledger:** productive assets, labor, inventory, insurance, working capital, unpaid time, and risk held outside the focal company.
- **Clock:** product life, inventory turn, contract maturity, refinancing date, replacement cycle, and technological obsolescence.
- **Adaptation:** ability to simplify, cannibalize, repurpose knowledge, and finance transition from durable cashflow.

## Customer-contract test

Treat customer trust and retained customer surplus as productive capital:

- A durable rent leaves enough value for repeat purchase, resale value, and voluntary loyalty.
- Maximum extraction through variants, complexity, financing, lock-in, or hidden fees can raise peak margin while shortening rent duration.
- Loss of default status appears first in delayed replacement, channel switching, resale value, incentives, and the installed base—not only reported revenue.
- A resilient incumbent may have forgone peak extraction, preserved simpler operations, and accumulated adjacent technical knowledge. Cash alone is not the moat.

## Fragmentation patterns

### Household

Reconstruct one commitment stack against dependable net labor income:

- housing, energy, insurance, and necessary transport;
- cards, overdrafts, and BNPL schedules across providers;
- leases, device plans, subscriptions, and automatic renewals;
- variable gig income net of vehicle, maintenance, insurance, energy, waiting time, and depreciation.

The risk is many low-salience claims with different due dates hitting one pay cycle. Second jobs, revolving balances, lost discretionary cash, cancellations, and synchronized cuts can precede formal default.

Keep three views separate: the service consumed, cash payments due now, and the maturity schedule of future obligations. Example: a subscription charged to a card creates one service purchase; later principal repayment is financing settlement, not another service purchase. Interest and fees are extra costs. In a cashflow view include the actual due payment once, without subtracting again a subscription already included in baseline expenses. Contract counts are not unique borrowers; overlapping multi-provider loans are not merely provider adoption.

### Platform

Recombine the transaction:

- **consumer:** headline price, markup, service/delivery fees, membership, tip;
- **worker:** base pay, incentive, tip, unpaid time, self-funded productive assets;
- **supplier/restaurant/host:** commission, ads, discounts, inventory, premises, cleaning, maintenance, working capital;
- **platform:** access, data, ranking, payment, and allocation rules.

Platform ROIC is not system ROIC. Treat tipping as a shadow labor charge when it is requested before service, fills base-pay gaps, or is manipulated through interface placement.

### Property

Separate real improvement from a price-transfer chain:

- host/flipper supplies property and renovation capital;
- platform and intermediaries capture fees;
- fragmented nightly, cleaning, and service prices can obscure all-in cost;
- the next guest or mortgage borrower must validate expected rent or resale premium;
- occupancy, marketing time, price cuts, and investor purchases are quantity signals before default.

## Phase-matched counterexample test

A valid counterexample faces the same substitution, cost, or demand shock and shows:

- use or volume remains economically viable;
- cash conversion survives without rising discounts or financing support;
- the ecosystem remains viable after external costs;
- legacy obligations do not block adaptation;
- existing capabilities can be redirected before the old rent expires;
- customer trust and retained surplus support repeat demand.

A company still entering harvest is not a counterexample to one already in rent compression.

## Cross-pillar bridge

Trace and classify every arrow:

```text
weaker labor/customer cashflow
→ financed or delayed consumption
→ stacked future claims
→ abrupt quantity adjustment
→ weaker company/host/project cashflow
→ lower collateral or debt coverage
→ lender/fund/lessor response
→ capex, hours, hiring, or credit cuts
→ weaker household cashflow
```

Use `proven`, `plausible`, or `absent`. Shared rate sensitivity is a correlation channel, not by itself proof of credit transmission.

## Direction-first writing rule

Lead with the mechanism and the economic bearer. Mention definitional or numerical qualifications only when they reverse the direction, change the phase, change the exposed party, or alter the transmission claim. Do not bury the useful conclusion under precision that leaves the decision unchanged.
